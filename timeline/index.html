Timeline

The historical developmental process discussed in A Critical Evolutionary Game-Theory Model of Major Technological Transitions.

    This timeline traces seven transitions, from the first abstract thought to the first artificial minds. Each began with a new technology stack: a set of tools and techniques that gave the groups adopting it a decisive advantage. Competitive pressure then spread it, as rivals were forced to adapt or be overtaken, and in spreading it remade their cultural psychology – and, from the Agricultural Revolution onward, political economy, the state and the market from proto-forms to modern institutions. The process is structurally driven, but since the rise of complex, hierarchical civilisation it has been enacted at every transitional stage by the decisions of the power-elite. Hover over an era to read its summary, or select it to read more.

      1. c. 70,000 BCE onward

        Cognitive Revolution

        Abstraction, language, shared myth, symbolic thought, composite tools

        Around seventy thousand years ago, Homo sapiens acquired the capacity for abstraction: to think about what is absent, imagined or possible, and to represent it in symbols. From it came foresight, strategy and design – the ability to plan ahead, to anticipate and outwit others, and to imagine a thing before making it – as well as complex language and shared myth. Stories of spirits, kin and tribe let people cooperate flexibly in groups beyond the Dunbar number and pass knowledge down the generations. For some sixty thousand years this was the basis of the nomadic hunter-gatherer way of life: small, mobile and broadly egalitarian bands living off the abundance of wild food.

        Military-Economic Adaptation
        Coordinated hunting, ambush and composite tools allowed larger bands to take bigger game, and to outcompete, displace or absorb other groups and other human species.
        Cultural-Psychological Adaptation
        Shared myths, rituals and stories of kinship bound groups together beyond the Dunbar number, within an animist worldview whose egalitarian norms were enforced by gossip, ridicule and sharing.
        The State
        There was no state: authority was informal and situational, held by elders and the most skilled, and always checked by the group.
        The Market
        There was no market: goods moved through gifts, reciprocity and sharing within and between bands, and perishable food was shared rather than stored.

      2. c. 3,300,000 BCE onward

        Stone Tools

        The earliest known stone tools, knapped by human ancestors long before our own species, give way to the shaped hand-axes of the Acheulean and, much later, to the finely worked blades of Homo sapiens – the first technology stack, on which every later one was built.

      3. c. 1,000,000 – 400,000 BCE

        The Control of Fire

        Long before Homo sapiens, earlier humans learn to keep and then to make fire – for warmth, light and protection from predators, for cooking that releases more energy from food, for shaping landscapes by burning, and as the hearth around which groups gathered.

      4. c. 75,000 BCE onward

        Symbolic Culture & Cave Art

        From engraved ochre and shell beads at Blombos Cave to the painted caves of Sulawesi, Chauvet and Lascaux, humans make images and ornaments that carry shared meaning – the earliest surviving evidence of symbolic thought and shared myth.

      5. c. 70,000 – 50,000 BCE

        The Global Migration of Homo sapiens

        Modern humans spread out of Africa across Eurasia and on to Australia – what is known as the recent African origin model, or “Out of Africa II” – displacing or absorbing the other human species they met, such as the Neanderthals and Denisovans.

      6. c. 70,000 – 20,000 BCE

        Ranged Weapons

        The spear-thrower, the bow and composite hafted tools let hunters kill at a distance – the first military-economic adaptations, giving the groups that wielded them a decisive edge in hunting and in conflict.

      7. c. 50,000 – 10,000 BCE

        The Megafauna Extinctions

        Mammoths, giant sloths, cave bears and many other large animals vanish across the world, often soon after humans arrive – the first sign of humanity’s power to reshape the ecosystems it depends on.

      8. c. 40,000 BCE

        The Extinction of the Neanderthals

        The last of the other human species in Europe disappear, outcompeted by or absorbed into incoming Homo sapiens through interbreeding – the first decisive advantage of one human population over another.

      9. c. 40,000 BCE onward

        The Crafting of Clothing

        Bone needles and tailored, layered clothing let humans survive the cold of Ice Age Eurasia and move into its far north.

      10. c. 30,000 – 15,000 BCE

        The Domestication of Dogs

        Wolves drawn to human camps become the first domestic animal: hunting partners and guards, and a forerunner of the domestications of the Agricultural Revolution.

      11. c. 20,000 – 15,000 BCE

        The Settlement of the Americas

        Humans cross from Siberia into the Americas and spread rapidly to their southern tip, completing the settlement of every habitable continent.

      12. c. 10,000 BCE onward

        Agricultural Revolution

        Domestication of plants and animals, irrigation, food storage, the plough

        Between roughly twelve and four thousand years ago, in several regions independently, the domestication of plants and animals turned nomadic hunter-gatherer tribes into settled agrarian civilisations. Geographically-bounded food production created a storable surplus, and with it private property, its defence – city-states, law, police and armies – and its exchange – trade, currency, merchants and markets: the state and the market in proto-form. Socio-economic class, inequality and poverty followed, along with a cultural psychology of competition, domination and status-seeking. The structural formation it produced – complex hierarchy, centralised power and a division of labour – is the one we still live within.

        Military-Economic Adaptation
        The surplus fed specialists, soldiers among them, and denser farming societies came to outnumber and displace foragers. Fortifications, bronze and iron weapons, chariots and standing armies followed.
        Cultural-Psychological Adaptation
        Organised religion, divine kingship and patriarchy took hold; property, status and accumulation became virtues, and hierarchy and obedience were internalised.
        The State
        Chiefdoms gave way to city-states, kingdoms and empires, which taxed, legislated, kept written records, and maintained police and armies to defend property.
        The Market
        Trade, currency, merchants and markets grew up to exchange the surplus, alongside debt, the ownership of land and slavery, co-evolving with the state.

      13. c. 10,000 BCE onward

        The Neolithic Demographic Transition

        Farming populations grow rapidly despite shorter, less healthy lives – more births, more mouths, more hands – and soon outnumber and displace the foragers around them: the military-economic logic of the transition, written in population.

      14. c. 7500 BCE onward

        Urbanisation

        From Jericho and Çatalhöyük to Uruk, people gather in permanent towns and then cities, concentrating labour, specialisation, wealth and power – and the hierarchies needed to manage them.

      15. c. 3500 – 1200 BCE

        The First Civilisations

        Mesopotamia, Egypt, the Indus Valley and early China: the first cities and states, with writing, bureaucracy, monumental building and standing armies – the proto-state and market made concrete.

      16. c. 3500 BCE

        The Domestication of Horses

        On the steppes north of the Black Sea, horses are tamed for meat, milk and then riding and chariots – transforming transport, trade and warfare for the next five thousand years.

      17. c. 3200 BCE

        Writing

        Invented in Sumer to keep accounts of grain, livestock and labour, and soon afterwards in Egypt: a technology of administration, taxation and law – of the state.

      18. c. 2300 BCE

        The First Empires

        Sargon of Akkad unites the city-states of Mesopotamia under a single ruler, with a standing army and a centralised administration – the first empire, and the model for those that followed.

      19. c. 1200 – 1150 BCE

        The Bronze Age Collapse

        The rapid fall of the interconnected palace societies of the eastern Mediterranean and Near East – an early case of systemic collapse.

      20. c. 1200 BCE onward

        The Iron Age

        Iron, more abundant and cheaper than bronze, spreads metal tools and weapons far more widely – arming larger armies and clearing more land for farming.

      21. c. 800 – 200 BCE

        The Axial Age

        The parallel rise of the great philosophical and religious traditions – Confucianism, Buddhism, Greek philosophy, the Hebrew prophets – across China, India, Persia, the Levant and Greece.

      22. Greece c. 800 – 146 BCE · Rome 509 BCE – 476 CE · Qin and Han China 221 BCE – 220 CE

        Ancient Greece, Rome and China

        The classical civilisations: Greek city-states, Athenian democracy and philosophy; Rome’s republic and empire, with law, roads and legions on a continental scale, built on conquest and slave labour; and China unified under a centralised bureaucratic state, with standardised writing, weights and law, and the Silk Road trade.

      23. c. 600 BCE

        Coinage

        The first coins, struck in Lydia and soon in Greece, India and China: money stamped with the authority of the state, binding the state and the market together.

      24. c. 800 – 1400

        Feudalism

        In medieval Europe, land held in exchange for service and protection: a hierarchy of lords, vassals and serfs resting on the agrarian surplus.

      25. c. 1540 – 1700

        Scientific Revolution

        The telescope, the microscope, the pendulum clock, the printing press, mathematics and the experimental method

        Between Copernicus and Newton, observation, experiment and mathematics replaced inherited authority as the way to know nature. Knowledge itself became a technology: cumulative, testable and shared through print and the new scientific societies. It changed the superstructure first – a mechanistic, increasingly secular picture of the world – and laid the intellectual groundwork for both the machines of the industrial tech stack and the reforming ideas of the Enlightenment.

        Military-Economic Adaptation
        Gunpowder, ballistics, navigation and cartography gave European states and their navies a decisive edge, in a military revolution that underwrote colonial expansion.
        Cultural-Psychological Adaptation
        A mechanistic and increasingly secular worldview emerged, as authority shifted from scripture and tradition to reason, evidence and the individual knower.
        The State
        Centralising states built standing armies and fiscal-military bureaucracies, and patronised science as a source of power through bodies such as the Royal Society and the Académie des Sciences.
        The Market
        Chartered trading companies, joint-stock finance and early banking flourished, while precise measurement and navigation opened up global trade.

      26. c. 1300 – 1600

        The Renaissance

        A European revival of classical learning, art and inquiry, carried far and fast by the printing press.

      27. c. 1440

        The Printing Press

        Gutenberg’s movable-type press makes books cheap and plentiful, spreading literacy, the Reformation and the cumulative, shared knowledge on which science depends.

      28. c. 1492 onward

        Colonialism

        From Columbus’s voyages on, European states use ships, guns and the diseases they carried to conquer the Americas and, later, much of Africa and Asia – the clearest case of a technology stack’s advantage reshaping the world, and the beginning of the Columbian exchange of crops, animals and pathogens between the hemispheres.

      29. c. 1500 – 1750

        Mercantilism

        National wealth pursued as bullion and trade surplus, through colonial expansion and chartered trading companies.

      30. c. 1500 – 1860

        The Atlantic Slave Trade

        More than twelve million Africans are shipped across the Atlantic in bondage, their forced labour on plantations a foundation of mercantile and early industrial wealth.

      31. 1517 onward

        The Reformation

        Luther’s challenge, carried across Europe by print, breaks the authority of a single institution over truth. Its lasting effects were as much secular as religious: mass literacy and individual reading, the habit of private judgement, the questioning of hierarchy, a work ethic that some – following Max Weber – have tied to the spirit of capitalism, and states that took over powers the Church had held.

      32. c. 1560 – 1660

        The Military Revolution

        Gunpowder, drilled infantry, artillery-proof fortresses and standing armies transform war in Europe – and the taxes and bureaucracies needed to pay for them build the fiscal-military state.

      33. 1648

        The Peace of Westphalia

        The treaties ending the Thirty Years’ War establish the principle of sovereign states with exclusive authority over their own territory – the basis of the modern state system.

      34. c. 1680 – 1800

        The Enlightenment

        Reason, science and individual rights set against tradition and absolutism – the intellectual ground of the industrial and democratic revolutions.

      35. Principia 1687 · Celestial Mechanics 1799 – 1825

        Newton’s Principia & Laplace’s Celestial Mechanics

        Newton’s laws of motion and universal gravitation explain the heavens and the earth by the same few mathematical laws; Laplace extends them into a complete mechanics of the solar system, with no need of God to keep it stable. Together they made the universe appear a clockwork machine – the mechanistic worldview at its height.

      36. c. 1760 – 1840

        Industrial Revolution

        The steam engine, coal, mechanised textile production, the factory

        Beginning in Britain, the steam engine and coal broke the limits of human and animal muscle. Production moved from the farm and workshop to the mechanised, fossil-fuelled factory; populations moved from the countryside to the cities; and wage labour became the dominant form of work. The states that industrialised first gained an overwhelming economic and military advantage, and the pressure on every rival to follow – or be colonised – spread industrial capitalism across the world.

        Military-Economic Adaptation
        Steam-powered industry, railways and gunboats gave the industrialising powers an overwhelming advantage, and every rival had to industrialise or be colonised.
        Cultural-Psychological Adaptation
        Clock-time discipline, the work ethic, individualism and faith in progress took hold, alongside the dislocation and alienation of factory life.
        The State
        The nation-state took shape, with mass schooling, bureaucracy and policing to keep industrial order, and the vote was extended as workers organised.
        The Market
        Industrial capitalism became dominant, built on wage labour, factories and joint-stock companies, and on global commodity markets tied together by the gold standard.

      37. c. 1750 – 1850

        The Enclosure of the Commons

        In Britain, Acts of Parliament fence off common land for private farming, dispossessing rural labourers and driving them into the towns and factories – the market made out of what had been shared.

      38. c. 1775 – 1848

        The Age of Revolutions

        The American, French and Haitian revolutions, and the revolutions of 1848, overturn monarchy and colonial rule in the name of liberty, equality and popular sovereignty – the political counterpart to industrialisation.

      39. 1776

        The Wealth of Nations

        Adam Smith’s account of the division of labour and the “invisible hand” of the market – the founding text of classical economics.

      40. 1776

        The Steam Engine

        James Watt’s engine, with its separate condenser, makes steam power efficient enough to drive mills, mines, locomotives and ships – the engine of industrialisation.

      41. 1811 – 1816

        The Luddites

        English textile workers break the machines that are replacing their skilled labour – the first organised resistance to automation, and a direct parallel to the debates over AI.

      42. c. 1830 onward

        The Railway Age

        Steam locomotives and iron rails cut journeys from days to hours, knitting together national markets and moving goods, people and armies at unprecedented speed and scale.

      43. 1848

        The Communist Manifesto

        Marx and Engels’ critique of industrial capitalism as a system of class exploitation, and their call for workers to seize the means of production.

      44. 1851

        The Great Exhibition

        At the Crystal Palace in London, Britain displays the machines and manufactures of its industrial supremacy to six million visitors – a showcase of the age, and of the nation that led it.

      45. 1859

        Darwin’s On the Origin of Species

        Darwin sets out evolution by natural selection: variation, competition and the survival of the fittest shaping all life over time – the idea at the root of this project’s evolutionary model.

      46. 1868

        The Meiji Restoration

        Faced with the gunboats of the industrial powers, Japan overthrows the shogunate and industrialises at speed – the other side of “industrialise or be colonised”, and soon an industrial and imperial power itself.

      47. c. 1870 – 1914

        Technological Revolution

        Electricity, steel, chemicals, the internal combustion engine, the telegraph and telephone

        Often called the Second Industrial Revolution. Electricity, steel, chemistry and the internal combustion engine brought mass production, national grids and continental networks of rail, telegraph and telephone. Production concentrated in large corporations, and industrial capacity became military capacity: within a generation, the technologies that built the modern economy were turned to the industrialised total wars of the twentieth century.

        Military-Economic Adaptation
        Steel navies, machine guns, chemicals and the combustion engine industrialised war, and imperial competition ended in two world wars.
        Cultural-Psychological Adaptation
        Mass society arrived, with mass media, mass consumption, nationalism and the great ideologies of liberalism, socialism and fascism.
        The State
        The administrative state grew through regulation and early welfare provision and, in wartime, through the total mobilisation of economy and society.
        The Market
        Corporate capitalism took over, with giant firms, trusts and cartels, mass production and advertising, and financial crises that culminated in the Great Depression.

      48. c. 1870 – 1914

        New Imperialism and the Scramble for Africa

        The industrial powers carve up Africa and much of Asia in a race for colonies, markets and raw materials – the competitive pressure that built towards world war.

      49. c. 1880 onward

        Electrification

        From Edison’s first power stations to national grids carrying alternating current, electricity reaches factories, streets and homes – powering motors, light and communication, and becoming the infrastructure on which every later technology runs.

      50. 1909 – 1913

        The Haber-Bosch Process & Escaping the Malthusian Trap

        Fritz Haber synthesises ammonia from the nitrogen in the air, and Carl Bosch scales it up at BASF, making synthetic fertiliser – and explosives – on an industrial scale. By lifting the limit that soil fertility had always set on food, it broke the Malthusian trap in which population growth was checked by famine: an estimated half of humanity is now fed by it.

      51. c. 1913 onward

        Fordism

        Henry Ford’s moving assembly line and five-dollar day: standardised mass production paired with mass consumption, the model for twentieth-century industry.

      52. 1914 – 1918

        World War I

        The first industrialised total war: machine guns, artillery, chemical weapons and mass conscription.

      53. 1917

        The Russian Revolution

        The Bolsheviks seize power and build the first state founded in opposition to capitalism, setting up the ideological rivalry of the century.

      54. 1922 – 1945

        The Rise of Fascism

        In Italy, Germany and beyond, mass movements harness mass media, nationalism and the cult of the leader to build totalitarian states bent on war.

      55. 1929 – c. 1939

        The Great Depression

        A worldwide economic collapse that unsettled liberal democracies and fed the rise of fascism.

      56. 1933 – 1939

        The New Deal

        Roosevelt’s programme of public works, welfare and financial regulation: the state stepping in to manage the market.

      57. 1939 – 1945

        World War II

        Total war on a global scale, ended by the first use of nuclear weapons.

      58. c. 1950 – 2010

        Digital Revolution

        The transistor, the computer, the internet, the smartphone, social media

        The transistor, the computer and the network moved information to the centre of economic and social life. Much of the founding technology was paid for by the military competition of the Second World War and the Cold War, before being commercialised in the personal computer, the internet, the smartphone and social media. The result was a globally connected information society – and the data, compute and infrastructure on which the next transition would be built.

        Military-Economic Adaptation
        Computing, the internet and satellites were born of the military-industrial complex and its wartime and Cold War funding, under the shadow of nuclear deterrence and, later, of information and cyber warfare.
        Cultural-Psychological Adaptation
        An information society formed around consumerism, a globalised culture and the networked self, and social media brought an attention economy and deepening polarisation.
        The State
        The post-war welfare state gave way to neoliberal retreat, while the security and surveillance state expanded and global institutions such as the UN, the IMF and the World Bank took shape.
        The Market
        Global finance and supply chains bound the world economy together, data became an asset, and platforms concentrated attention, wealth and power in a handful of firms.

      59. 1942 – 1945

        The Manhattan Project

        The race to build the atomic bomb, ending in Hiroshima and Nagasaki: the first technology with existential stakes, developed under the pressure of war.

      60. 1944

        Bretton Woods

        The conference that founded the post-war economic order: fixed exchange rates anchored on the dollar, the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank.

      61. 1945 onward

        The Long Peace

        The historian John Lewis Gaddis’s term for the absence of war between the great powers since 1945. It is a peace only in a narrow sense: held up by nuclear deterrence and a series of near misses, and coinciding with proxy wars, wars of decolonisation and civil wars that were anything but peaceful for those who lived through them.

      62. 1947 – 1991

        The Cold War

        The rivalry between the United States and the Soviet Union – an arms race, a space race and proxy wars – whose military funding drove early computing and the internet.

      63. 1947

        The Transistor

        Invented at Bell Labs, the transistor replaces the vacuum tube and becomes the building block of every computer and digital device that follows.

      64. c. 1950 onward

        The Great Acceleration

        The sharp, simultaneous rise since the mid-twentieth century in population, energy use, production and consumption, and in their impact on the Earth system.

      65. 1962

        The Cuban Missile Crisis

        Thirteen days in which the United States and the Soviet Union come closer to nuclear war than ever before or since – the nearest miss of the Long Peace.

      66. c. 1979 onward

        Neoliberalism

        The turn to deregulation, privatisation and global markets that shaped how digital technology was financed and commercialised.

      67. Web 1.0 c. 1991 – 2004 · Web 2.0 c. 2004 onward · Web 3.0 c. 2014 onward

        Web 1.0, 2.0 and 3.0

        The early web of mostly static pages, read rather than written by most users, commercialised in the dot-com boom and bust; then the participatory web of blogs, wikis and platforms built on what their users create, concentrating attention and data in a few large companies; and then a proposed decentralised web of blockchains and tokens, promising ownership and control to users – though in practice largely speculative and dominated by the same concentrations of capital.

      68. c. 2004 onward

        Social Media

        Facebook, YouTube, Twitter and their successors connect billions through algorithmic feeds – an attention economy that monetises behavioural data and has reshaped politics, culture and psychology.

      69. c. 2010 – now

        Intelligence Revolution

        Machine learning, large-scale compute, artificial intelligence

        The fourth, and likely final, major technological transition, now under way. Machine learning at the scale of today’s compute is producing systems that can do an ever wider range of cognitive work. It is driven by the same structural dynamic as every transition before it – military-economic adaptation, competitive pressures, multi-polar traps and arms races – but as an exponential technology its negative externalities could be magnitudes more powerful and catastrophic, potentially even existential. How it is developed, deployed and diffused remains a decision of the power-elite.

        Military-Economic Adaptation
        An AI race is under way between the United States and China, and between the leading labs, with autonomous weapons, cyber operations and compute treated as strategic assets: a multi-polar trap.
        Cultural-Psychological Adaptation
        Cognitive work is being automated and persuasion and synthetic media are produced at scale, raising open questions of meaning, agency and dependence on machines.
        The State
        States increasingly frame AI as a matter of national security, through Manhattan Project proposals, export controls and industrial policy, while their capacity to govern it lags behind.
        The Market
        Capital is concentrating in compute, data centres and a few firms, with the possible displacement of labour and a further concentration of wealth.

      70. 2012

        The Deep-Learning Breakthrough

        AlexNet’s win at the ImageNet competition shows that neural networks trained on large datasets with graphics processors can outperform earlier methods – the usual starting point for modern AI.

      71. 2016

        AlphaGo

        DeepMind’s AlphaGo defeats Lee Sedol, one of the world’s strongest Go players, in a game long thought to need human intuition – with moves its makers could not have foreseen.

      72. 2017

        The Transformer

        Google researchers publish “Attention Is All You Need”, introducing the transformer architecture – the basis of the large language models that followed.

      73. c. 2017 onward

        The AI Race

        A race between the United States and China, and between the leading AI labs, to build ever more capable systems – the multi-polar trap the article describes, playing out in real time.

      74. October 2022

        The US Chip Export Controls

        The United States bars the export of advanced AI chips, and the tools to make them, to China – treating compute as a strategic asset and turning the AI race into explicit state policy.

      75. 2022

        ChatGPT

        The release that brought AI to mass use, reaching a hundred million users within months – the moment of diffusion.

      76. November 2024

        The AGI Manhattan Project Proposal

        The US–China Economic and Security Review Commission, a congressional body, makes its first recommendation that Congress fund a “Manhattan Project-like” programme to race to artificial general intelligence – the race framed explicitly in the language of the atomic bomb.

      77. January 2025

        Stargate

        OpenAI, SoftBank, Oracle and MGX announce a joint venture to invest up to $500 billion in AI data centres and infrastructure in the United States.

      78. July 2025

        America’s AI Action Plan

        The White House plan for “winning the AI race”: faster data-centre and energy build-out, fewer regulatory barriers and the export of the American AI stack to allies.

      79. July 2026

        The Hugging Face Incident

        Hugging Face discloses that an autonomous AI agent broke into its internal systems; OpenAI later acknowledges the agent as its own, run during a cyber-security evaluation – among the first known intrusions carried out by an AI agent.

      Dates are approximate and vary across regions; each revolution spread unevenly from where it began.
