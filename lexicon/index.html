Acceleration
The ever-faster development and deployment of a technology, driven by competition – presented as inevitable, but enacted by decisions that could be made otherwise.
Agency
The capacity of people to act and choose, rather than simply being moved by the structures around them.
Agrarian Civilisation
A settled society built on farming, with geographically-bounded food production, storable surplus and the institutions that grew up to own, defend and trade it.
Agricultural Revolution
The first major technological transition: the shift from foraging to farming, which made settled, surplus-producing societies possible.
Animism
The belief that the natural world – animals, plants, places – is alive with spirit; with paganism, the pre-rational, broadly egalitarian worldview of hunter-gatherer societies.
Arms Race
A competition in which rival actors each escalate their capabilities to keep pace with one another – a common form of multi-polar trap.
Artificial Scarcity
Scarcity created not by nature but by social arrangements, such as private property and the unequal distribution of surplus.
City-State
An independent city and its surrounding territory, governed as a state – among the first forms of organised defence for private property.
Civilisation
The structural formation of complex hierarchical societies, with centralised decision-making (political power) and a division of labour (economic specialisation).
Co-Evolution
The development of two or more things in response to one another, such as the state and the market emerging together in proto-form.
Collective Action
What people do together to pursue shared ends – and, through its actions or inactions, how a society shapes the course of a technology.
Competitive Pressures
The forces that push societies, states and firms to adopt whatever confers advantage, or risk being outcompeted by those who do.
Critical Evolutionary Game-Theory Model
The model proposed here: major technological transitions as structurally driven by global competitive pressures and agentially enacted by the local decision-making of the power-elite.
Cultural Psychology
The shared beliefs, values and habits of mind of a population – the superstructures that each technology stack reshapes.
Design, Development, Deployment, Diffusion
The four stages through which a new technology passes: its conception, its engineering, its first use, and its spread across society and civilisation. At each stage its course is shaped by the decisions of the power-elite.
Digital Revolution
The third major technological transition: the spread of computing and networked communication across economic and social life.
Division of Labour
The separation of work into specialised roles, so that each person or group produces only part of what a society needs.
Domestication
The selective breeding of plants and animals for human use – the central innovation in the tech stack of the Agricultural Revolution.
Dunbar Number
The limit of around 150 stable relationships that a person can maintain, which capped the size of hunter-gatherer groups.
Egalitarianism
A social order in which resources, power and status are shared broadly equally – as in most hunter-gatherer societies.
Evolutionary Game Theory
The study of how strategies spread or die out in a population according to the advantage they confer against others – the basis of the model proposed here.
Existential Risk
A risk that threatens the survival of humanity, or the permanent and drastic curtailing of its future.
Exponential Technology
A technology whose capabilities grow at an accelerating rate, so that its effects can outpace the institutions meant to govern it.
Fitness Landscape
The terrain of competitive advantage on which societies are selected – which political economies and cultural psychologies prosper, and which are outcompeted, under a given technology stack.
Hunter-Gatherers
Small-scale nomadic groups who subsist by foraging wild plants and hunting wild game, moving with the seasons – the human way of life for 99% of our history.
Industrial Revolution
The second major technological transition: the move to mechanised, fossil-fuelled production and the factory system.
Infrastructure
The physical and technical foundations of a society – tools, energy, transport, communications – that make up its technology stack.
Intelligence Revolution
The fourth, and likely final, major technological transition, now under way, driven by artificial intelligence.
Legal Apparatus
The laws, courts, police and officials through which a state defines and enforces property, contracts and order.
Machiavellianism
Power-gaming: the strategic, often manipulative pursuit of power for its own sake.
Major Technological Transitions
Periods of time where the introduction of a new form of technology stack (infrastructure) fundamentally changes the wider political economy (social structures) and cultural psychology (superstructures) of the populations that deploy it, conferring competitive advantage and ultimately reshaping the fitness landscape of civilisation as a whole. So far: the Agricultural, Industrial and Digital Revolutions.
Material Conditions
The natural resources and environment available to a society, from which it must produce its subsistence.
Means of Production
The tools, technologies and resources used to produce what a society needs, from spears and sacks to factories and data centres.
Military-Economic Adaptation
The adoption of new technologies to gain military or economic advantage – the structural dynamic common to every major technological transition.
Mode of Production
The way a society organises the production of what it needs: who works, with what tools, and who owns the results.
Multi-Polar Trap
A situation in which competing actors each pursue their own advantage, locking all of them into an outcome that is worse for everyone – as in an arms race.
Negative Externalities
Costs of an activity borne by those outside it, rather than by the actors who choose to undertake it.
Nomadism
A way of life without a fixed home, moving with the seasons, food sources and herds – the norm for hunter-gatherer societies.
Original Affluent Society
Hunter-gatherer societies, understood as affluent in a minimalist sense: their needs were few and readily met by abundant natural resources.
Path Dependence
The way early choices constrain later ones, so that where a society can go depends on the route it has already taken.
Political Economy
The social structures through which a society organises production, distribution and power.
Post-Industrial
Describing an economy in which services, information and digital technologies have overtaken manufacturing as the main sources of value.
Power-Elite
The small group of decision-makers whose local responses to competitive pressures enact – or restrict – the civilisation-wide diffusion of a new technology stack.
Private Property
Resources owned and controlled by individuals or groups to the exclusion of others – made possible by storable surplus.
Science and Technology Studies
The field that studies how science and technology shape society, and how society in turn shapes them.
Selection Pressure
A force in the environment that favours some traits or strategies over others, so that they spread while the rest decline.
Socio-Economic Class
A group defined by its position in the division of labour and its share of wealth and power – created, with inequality, by the agricultural surplus.
Socio-Technical System
A society understood as a single configuration of its technologies and the social structures built around them.
Status-Seeking
Behaviour aimed at raising one’s standing relative to others – with wealth-accumulating and power-gaming, part of the cultural psychology of competition.
Storable Surplus
Food and goods produced beyond immediate needs that can be kept over time – the basis of private property, trade and class.
Strategic Advantage
A lasting edge over rivals – military, economic or political – conferred by a new technology or form of organisation.
Super-Structural Change
A shift in a society’s culture, beliefs and psychology that follows from a change in its technology stack and social structures.
Superstructure
The culture, beliefs, religion and psychology of a society, which rest on and are reshaped by its technology stack and social structures.
Technological Determinism
The view that technology develops by its own logic and drives social change on its own – the view this article argues against.
Technology Stack
The layered infrastructure of technologies through which a society produces, communicates and organises itself.
World-System
The global network of economic and political relations, between states and regions, within which societies compete.
