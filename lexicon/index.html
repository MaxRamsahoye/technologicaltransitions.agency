Curated Terms

Major Technological Transitions
Periods of time where the introduction of a new form of technology stack (infrastructure) fundamentally changes the wider political economy (social structures) and cultural psychology (superstructures) of the populations that deploy it, conferring competitive advantage and ultimately reshaping the fitness landscape of civilisation as a whole. So far: the Agricultural, Industrial and Digital Revolutions.
Tech Stack (Infrastructure)
The layered infrastructure of technologies through which a society produces, communicates and organises itself.
Political Economy (Social Structure)
The social structures through which a society organises production, distribution and power.
Cultural Psychology (Superstructure)
The shared beliefs, values and habits of mind of a population – the superstructures that each technology stack reshapes.
Military-Economic Adaptationism
Allan Dafoe’s account of how technology drives social change: in a competitive international system, societies that adopt technologies conferring military and economic advantage prosper, while those that do not are outcompeted, conquered or forced to follow.
Accelerationism
The view that the development of technology, and of the capitalism driving it, should be sped up rather than restrained – whether to hasten its benefits or to push the existing order beyond itself.
Exterminism
E.P. Thompson’s term for the tendencies of a society – in its economy, its polity and its ideology – that thrust it in a direction whose outcome must be the extermination of multitudes: for him, the logic of the nuclear arms race.
Extractivism
An economy, or a way of developing one, built on taking natural resources – minerals, fossil fuels, timber, land and labour – in bulk for use elsewhere, with little return to the places and people they are taken from.
Systemic Existential Risk
An existential risk that arises not from a single catastrophe or bad actor but from the structure of a system as a whole – the interaction of competitive pressures, institutions and technologies that moves it, step by step, towards catastrophe.
Multi-Agent System
A system of many interacting agents – people, firms, states or machines – each pursuing its own ends, whose collective behaviour emerges from their interactions rather than from any one agent’s design.
Negative Externalities
Costs of an activity borne by those outside it, rather than by the actors who choose to undertake it.
Attractor States
The stable configurations a system tends to settle into, whatever its starting point – in civilisational terms, outcomes such as runaway arms races, collapse or lock-in, towards which competitive dynamics pull.
Macrohistory
The study of history at the largest scales of time and space – the long-run patterns of whole civilisations and eras, rather than particular events, people or places.
Civilisational Paradigm
The overall configuration of a civilisation in a given era – its technology stack, political economy and cultural psychology taken together, as in the agrarian, industrial or digital paradigms.
Civilisational Dynamics
The forces and feedbacks that move a civilisation through time – growth, competition, innovation, inequality, overreach and decline – carrying it from one paradigm towards the next, or towards collapse.
Civilisational Collapse
The rapid and lasting loss of a society’s complexity – its population, institutions, economy and capacity to govern – as befell the Western Roman Empire or the palace states of the Late Bronze Age.
Philosophy of Technology
The branch of philosophy that examines the nature of technology and its effects on human life and society – how tools shape the people who make and use them, and how they might be governed.
Technological Determinism
The view that technology develops by its own logic and drives social change on its own – the view this article argues against.
Social Construction of Technology
The view, set against technological determinism, that technologies are shaped by the social groups who design, contest and use them, rather than following an inevitable logic of their own.
Exponential Technology
A technology whose capabilities grow at an accelerating rate, so that its effects can outpace the institutions meant to govern it.
Critical Evolutionary Game-Theory
Evolutionary game theory made critical: the study of how strategies spread by the advantage they confer, joined to an account of the agents who choose them – the basis of the model proposed here, in which major technological transitions are structurally driven by global competitive pressures and agentially enacted by the power-elite.
Evolutionary Prisoner’s Dilemma
The prisoner’s dilemma played over and over across a population, in which cooperating and defecting strategies spread according to their payoffs – showing how defection can come to dominate even when every player would gain from cooperation.
Security Dilemma (also Spiral Model)
The predicament in which one state’s efforts to make itself secure – arming, allying, fortifying – make its rivals less secure, so that they respond in kind and all end up less safe. The spiral model describes how this mutual fear escalates towards arms races and war.
Multi-Polar Trap
A situation in which competing actors each pursue their own advantage, locking all of them into an outcome that is worse for everyone – as in an arms race.
Darwinian Trap & Hobbesian Trap
Two names for the bind a multi-polar trap creates. In a Darwinian trap, selection rewards a strategy that serves the individual but harms the whole, so that it spreads anyway; in a Hobbesian trap, mutual fear of attack drives each party to arm, or to strike, first.
Arms Race
A competition in which rival actors each escalate their capabilities to keep pace with one another – a common form of multi-polar trap.
Red Queen Race
An arms race in which each side must keep improving merely to hold its place against the others – after the Red Queen in Through the Looking-Glass, who must run as fast as she can to stay where she is.
World-System
The global network of economic and political relations, between states and regions, within which societies compete.
Nation-States & Nationalism
The modern form of the state – sovereign and territorial, claiming to embody a single nation – and the ideology that binds a population to it, mobilising its loyalty, taxes and soldiers for competition with other states.
Corporations & Techno-Capitalism
The large, legally immortal firms that organise production and capital across borders, and the stage of capitalism in which technological innovation itself becomes the main source of profit and power.
Hyper-Agents
Actors – states, corporations and, increasingly, AI systems – whose scale, resources and reach let them act on the world-system far beyond any individual, shaping the dynamics within which everyone else must act.
Leviathan
Thomas Hobbes’s image of the sovereign state as an artificial giant made up of its subjects, holding a monopoly of force to end the war of all against all.
Goliath
Luke Kemp’s name for dominance hierarchies – states and empires that grow through extraction and elite competition until they become fragile and fall.
Moloch
The ancient god of child sacrifice, taken – after Allen Ginsberg’s poem Howl and Scott Alexander’s essay Meditations on Moloch – as a name for the logic of the multi-polar trap: the competitive dynamic that sacrifices every other value to winning, though no one chooses it.
Megamachine
Lewis Mumford’s term for the organisation of vast numbers of people into a single hierarchical machine – first in the armies and labour forces of the ancient god-kings, then in the military-industrial complexes of the modern world.
Technium
Kevin Kelly’s term for the whole of technology taken as a single, self-reinforcing system, which grows and evolves with tendencies of its own.
Economic Superorganism
Nate Hagens’s term for the global economy as a single energy-hungry organism, driven towards profit and growth beyond the control of any of the people within it.
Civilisational Risk
A risk that threatens civilisation as a whole – its institutions, knowledge and capacity to sustain itself – rather than any one people or place, whether through sudden catastrophe or gradual decline.
Catastrophic Risk
A risk of an event that would kill a large share of humanity or cripple civilisation worldwide – such as nuclear war, a pandemic or the failure of critical systems – without necessarily ending its future.
Existential Risk
A risk that threatens the survival of humanity, or the permanent and drastic curtailing of its future.
Disempowerment Risk
The risk that humanity gradually loses the power to shape its own future – as AI systems take over economic, political and cultural roles, and the institutions that once depended on human participation no longer need it – without any single catastrophe.
AI Risk
The risks posed by advanced artificial intelligence – from misuse, accidents and the concentration of power to systems pursuing goals at odds with human interests – up to and including the existential.
Technological Revolutions
The eight turning points traced on the Timeline, in each of which a new technology stack gave the groups adopting it a decisive advantage and, as it spread, remade their political economy and cultural psychology.
Cognitive Revolution
From around seventy thousand years ago, the emergence in Homo sapiens of abstract thought, complex language and shared myth, which allowed flexible cooperation in large groups and gave rise to the nomadic hunter-gatherer way of life.
Agricultural Revolution
The first major technological transition: the shift from foraging to farming, which made settled, surplus-producing societies possible.
Urban Revolution
From around six thousand years ago, the growth of farming towns into the first cities, bringing writing, bronze and monumental building, and with them the state and the market in proto-form.
Scientific Revolution
The replacement, in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, of inherited authority by observation, experiment and mathematics as the way to know nature – laying the groundwork for industry and the Enlightenment.
Industrial Revolution
The second major technological transition: the move to mechanised, fossil-fuelled production and the factory system.
Technological Revolution
From the late nineteenth century, the age of electricity, steel, chemistry and the internal combustion engine, which brought mass production, continental networks and, within a generation, industrialised total war.
Digital Revolution
The third major technological transition: the spread of computing and networked communication across economic and social life.
Intelligence Revolution
The fourth, and likely final, major technological transition, now under way, driven by artificial intelligence.
Constraints vs Affordances
The limits a way of life imposes, and the new possibilities a technology stack opens up to overcome them – each major technological transition lifting a constraint that had bound every society before it.
Kinship Bonds vs Shared Myth (Cognitive)
Before abstract thought, cooperation reached only as far as kinship and personal acquaintance – the small band anyone could know; shared myths, gods and stories let strangers trust and cooperate with one another in far larger numbers.
Individual Experience vs Cumulative Culture (Cognitive)
What each person knew died with them, and every generation had to learn for itself; language let knowledge be taught, kept and built on from one generation to the next, so that culture could accumulate.
Natural Perishability vs Storable Surplus (Agricultural)
Before farming, food spoiled and could not be accumulated, which kept bands small, mobile and broadly equal; grain and livestock made a surplus that could be stored, owned and taxed, and with it came private property, hierarchy and the state.
Private Property vs Shared Land (Agricultural)
Among foragers, land was shared and used by all; with farming it became property – owned, inherited and fenced – a new constraint on the many who lost access to it, and the source of the surplus, power and protection of the state enjoyed by the few who held it.
Human Cognition vs Written Record (Urban)
What a society could know, count and command was limited by what its people could hold in mind and remember; writing kept taxes, laws, debts and accounts beyond any one memory, and so made administration on the scale of a state possible.
Kin Loyalty vs Codified Law (Urban)
Order was kept by custom and obligations of family and clan; written law, courts and kings bound strangers to a common authority, making possible societies far larger than any kin group.
Human Senses vs Scientific Instruments (Scientific)
Knowledge of nature was limited to what the unaided eye and hand could perceive; the telescope, microscope, clock and thermometer extended perception into the very distant, the very small and the precisely measured.
Scribal Copying vs Printed Knowledge (Scientific)
Knowledge copied by hand was scarce, slow to spread and easily corrupted; the printing press made it standardised, widely shared and cumulative – the precondition of a scientific community.
Malthusian Trap vs Mass-Food Production (Industrial)
For most of history, every gain in the food supply was swallowed by population growth, keeping most people near subsistence; mechanised farming, fossil fuels and synthetic fertiliser broke the trap, feeding populations many times larger.
Muscle Power vs Fossil Energy (Industrial)
Work was limited by the strength of people and animals and the reach of wind and water; coal and steam released the stored sunlight of millions of years, multiplying the energy at a society’s command.
Innate Needs vs Manufactured Desires (Technological)
Human needs are finite, and an economy that only met them would soon reach its limits; but mass production requires mass consumption, so the Technological Revolution brought advertising, branding and consumer credit to manufacture desire – turning finite needs into the artificially infinite wants of consumerism.
Pitched Battles vs Total War (Technological)
War was fought by professional armies in set engagements, with limited means and aims; industrialised war mobilised every factory, citizen and resource of a nation, and made the whole of society a target.
Physical Distance vs Instant Connection (Digital)
Communication was bound by the speed of travel and the post; networked computers put anyone in reach of anyone, instantly and across the world.
Human Calculation vs Machine Computation (Digital)
What could be calculated, modelled or controlled was limited by the speed of human thought and arithmetic; the computer made calculation effectively unlimited – the ground on which artificial intelligence is built.
Human Labour vs AI Agents (Intelligence)
Every economy until now has depended on human labour, physical and cognitive, which has given working people a measure of bargaining power; AI agents able to do cognitive work threaten to lift that constraint, and with it that power.
Human Oversight vs Autonomous Systems (Intelligence)
Decisions in war, markets and government were made, or at least checked, by people; autonomous AI systems act faster than any person can follow, removing human judgement from the loop.
Civilisational Epochs
Names proposed for the age in which human civilisation has become a force on the scale of the planet itself – each naming a different driver, from humanity as a whole to capital, death or machine intelligence.
The Anthropocene
The proposed geological epoch in which human activity has become the dominant influence on the Earth’s climate and ecosystems – dated by most from the Industrial Revolution, or from the Great Acceleration after 1945.
The Deep Anthropocene
The view that humanity’s reshaping of the planet began long before industry – with the extinctions of the megafauna, the use of fire and the spread of farming that followed the Cognitive and Agricultural Revolutions.
The Capitalocene
Jason W. Moore’s and Andreas Malm’s alternative to the Anthropocene, which locates the cause of planetary crisis not in humanity as a whole but in capitalism and its drive to accumulate by appropriating cheap nature and labour.
The Necrocene
Justin McBrien’s term for capitalism as a system that accumulates through extinction – the ‘new death’ of species, cultures and ecosystems on which its growth depends.
The Novacene
James Lovelock’s name for a coming age in which hyper-intelligent machines, born of AI, succeed humanity as the planet’s dominant form of intelligence.
Acceleration
The ever-faster development and deployment of a technology, driven by competition – presented as inevitable, but enacted by decisions that could be made otherwise.
Accelerationism
The view that the development of technology, and of the capitalism driving it, should be sped up rather than restrained – whether to hasten its benefits or to push the existing order beyond itself.
Agency
The capacity of people to act and choose, rather than simply being moved by the structures around them.
Agrarian Civilisation
A settled society built on farming, with geographically-bounded food production, storable surplus and the institutions that grew up to own, defend and trade it.
Agricultural Revolution
The first major technological transition: the shift from foraging to farming, which made settled, surplus-producing societies possible.
AI Agents
Artificial intelligence systems that pursue goals by taking actions on their own – planning, using tools and carrying out tasks – rather than only answering questions, and so able to stand in for human labour, cognitive work included.
AI Risk
The risks posed by advanced artificial intelligence – from misuse, accidents and the concentration of power to systems pursuing goals at odds with human interests – up to and including the existential.
Animism
The belief that the natural world – animals, plants, places – is alive with spirit; with paganism, the pre-rational, broadly egalitarian worldview of hunter-gatherer societies.
Anthropocene
The proposed geological epoch in which human activity has become the dominant influence on the Earth’s climate and ecosystems – dated by most from the Industrial Revolution, or from the Great Acceleration after 1945.
Arms Race
A competition in which rival actors each escalate their capabilities to keep pace with one another – a common form of multi-polar trap.
Artificial Scarcity
Scarcity created not by nature but by social arrangements, such as private property and the unequal distribution of surplus.
Attractor States
The stable configurations a system tends to settle into, whatever its starting point – in civilisational terms, outcomes such as runaway arms races, collapse or lock-in, towards which competitive dynamics pull.
Capitalocene
Jason W. Moore’s and Andreas Malm’s alternative to the Anthropocene, which locates the cause of planetary crisis not in humanity as a whole but in capitalism and its drive to accumulate by appropriating cheap nature and labour.
Catastrophic Risk
A risk of an event that would kill a large share of humanity or cripple civilisation worldwide – such as nuclear war, a pandemic or the failure of critical systems – without necessarily ending its future.
City-State
An independent city and its surrounding territory, governed as a state – among the first forms of organised defence for private property.
Civilisation
The structural formation of complex hierarchical societies, with centralised decision-making (political power) and a division of labour (economic specialisation).
Civilisational Collapse
The rapid and lasting loss of a society’s complexity – its population, institutions, economy and capacity to govern – as befell the Western Roman Empire or the palace states of the Late Bronze Age.
Civilisational Dynamics
The forces and feedbacks that move a civilisation through time – growth, competition, innovation, inequality, overreach and decline – carrying it from one paradigm towards the next, or towards collapse.
Civilisational Paradigm
The overall configuration of a civilisation in a given era – its technology stack, political economy and cultural psychology taken together, as in the agrarian, industrial or digital paradigms.
Civilisational Risk
A risk that threatens civilisation as a whole – its institutions, knowledge and capacity to sustain itself – rather than any one people or place, whether through sudden catastrophe or gradual decline.
Co-Evolution
The development of two or more things in response to one another, such as the state and the market emerging together in proto-form.
Cognitive Revolution
From around seventy thousand years ago, the emergence in Homo sapiens of abstract thought, complex language and shared myth, which allowed flexible cooperation in large groups and gave rise to the nomadic hunter-gatherer way of life.
Collective Action
What people do together to pursue shared ends – and, through its actions or inactions, how a society shapes the course of a technology.
Competitive Pressures
The forces that push societies, states and firms to adopt whatever confers advantage, or risk being outcompeted by those who do.
Corporation
A legal person, separate from its owners and able to outlive them, that pools capital and organises production on a scale no individual could – the principal economic actor of industrial and digital capitalism.
Critical Evolutionary Game-Theory Model
The model proposed here: major technological transitions as structurally driven by global competitive pressures and agentially enacted by the local decision-making of the power-elite.
Cultural Psychology
The shared beliefs, values and habits of mind of a population – the superstructures that each technology stack reshapes.
Darwinian Trap
A situation in which selection rewards a strategy that serves the individual but harms the whole, so that it spreads even as it leaves everyone worse off.
Deep Anthropocene
The view that humanity’s reshaping of the planet began long before industry – with the extinctions of the megafauna, the use of fire and the spread of farming that followed the Cognitive and Agricultural Revolutions.
Design, Development, Deployment, Diffusion
The four stages through which a new technology passes: its conception, its engineering, its first use, and its spread across society and civilisation. At each stage its course is shaped by the decisions of the power-elite.
Digital Revolution
The third major technological transition: the spread of computing and networked communication across economic and social life.
Disempowerment Risk
The risk that humanity gradually loses the power to shape its own future – as AI systems take over economic, political and cultural roles, and the institutions that once depended on human participation no longer need it – without any single catastrophe.
Division of Labour
The separation of work into specialised roles, so that each person or group produces only part of what a society needs.
Domestication
The selective breeding of plants and animals for human use – the central innovation in the tech stack of the Agricultural Revolution.
Dunbar Number
The limit of around 150 stable relationships that a person can maintain, which capped the size of hunter-gatherer groups.
Economic Superorganism
Nate Hagens’s term for the global economy as a single energy-hungry organism, driven towards profit and growth beyond the control of any of the people within it.
Egalitarianism
A social order in which resources, power and status are shared broadly equally – as in most hunter-gatherer societies.
Evolutionary Game Theory
The study of how strategies spread or die out in a population according to the advantage they confer against others – the basis of the model proposed here.
Evolutionary Prisoner’s Dilemma
The prisoner’s dilemma played over and over across a population, in which cooperating and defecting strategies spread according to their payoffs – showing how defection can come to dominate even when every player would gain from cooperation.
Existential Risk
A risk that threatens the survival of humanity, or the permanent and drastic curtailing of its future.
Exponential Technology
A technology whose capabilities grow at an accelerating rate, so that its effects can outpace the institutions meant to govern it.
Exterminism
E.P. Thompson’s term for the tendencies of a society – in its economy, its polity and its ideology – that thrust it in a direction whose outcome must be the extermination of multitudes: for him, the logic of the nuclear arms race.
Extractivism
An economy, or a way of developing one, built on taking natural resources – minerals, fossil fuels, timber, land and labour – in bulk for use elsewhere, with little return to the places and people they are taken from.
Fitness Landscape
The terrain of competitive advantage on which societies are selected – which political economies and cultural psychologies prosper, and which are outcompeted, under a given technology stack.
Goliath
Luke Kemp’s name for dominance hierarchies – states and empires that grow through extraction and elite competition until they become fragile and fall.
Hobbesian Trap
A situation in which mutual fear of attack drives each party to arm, or to strike, first – after Hobbes’s war of all against all, in which the fear of violence is itself its cause.
Hunter-Gatherers
Small-scale nomadic groups who subsist by foraging wild plants and hunting wild game, moving with the seasons – the human way of life for 99% of our history.
Hyper-Agents
Actors – states, corporations and, increasingly, AI systems – whose scale, resources and reach let them act on the world-system far beyond any individual, shaping the dynamics within which everyone else must act.
Industrial Revolution
The second major technological transition: the move to mechanised, fossil-fuelled production and the factory system.
Infrastructure
The physical and technical foundations of a society – tools, energy, transport, communications – that make up its technology stack.
Intelligence Revolution
The fourth, and likely final, major technological transition, now under way, driven by artificial intelligence.
Legal Apparatus
The laws, courts, police and officials through which a state defines and enforces property, contracts and order.
Leviathan
Thomas Hobbes’s image of the sovereign state as an artificial giant made up of its subjects, holding a monopoly of force to end the war of all against all.
Machiavellianism
Power-gaming: the strategic, often manipulative pursuit of power for its own sake.
Macrohistory
The study of history at the largest scales of time and space – the long-run patterns of whole civilisations and eras, rather than particular events, people or places.
Major Technological Transitions
Periods of time where the introduction of a new form of technology stack (infrastructure) fundamentally changes the wider political economy (social structures) and cultural psychology (superstructures) of the populations that deploy it, conferring competitive advantage and ultimately reshaping the fitness landscape of civilisation as a whole. So far: the Agricultural, Industrial and Digital Revolutions.
Malthusian Trap
The tendency, described by Thomas Malthus, for gains in the food supply to be absorbed by population growth, so that living standards stay near subsistence – broken only by industrial agriculture and the demographic transition.
Material Conditions
The natural resources and environment available to a society, from which it must produce its subsistence.
Means of Production
The tools, technologies and resources used to produce what a society needs, from spears and sacks to factories and data centres.
Megamachine
Lewis Mumford’s term for the organisation of vast numbers of people into a single hierarchical machine – first in the armies and labour forces of the ancient god-kings, then in the military-industrial complexes of the modern world.
Military-Economic Adaptation
The adoption of new technologies to gain military or economic advantage – the structural dynamic common to every major technological transition.
Mode of Production
The way a society organises the production of what it needs: who works, with what tools, and who owns the results.
Moloch
The ancient god of child sacrifice, taken – after Allen Ginsberg’s poem Howl and Scott Alexander’s essay Meditations on Moloch – as a name for the logic of the multi-polar trap: the competitive dynamic that sacrifices every other value to winning, though no one chooses it.
Multi-Agent System
A system of many interacting agents – people, firms, states or machines – each pursuing its own ends, whose collective behaviour emerges from their interactions rather than from any one agent’s design.
Multi-Polar Trap
A situation in which competing actors each pursue their own advantage, locking all of them into an outcome that is worse for everyone – as in an arms race.
Nation-State
A sovereign state whose territory and institutions claim to correspond to a single nation – the dominant form of the state since the age of the industrial and national revolutions.
Nationalism
The ideology that a people forms a nation with a right to its own state, binding loyalty to that state and mobilising its population for competition with others.
Necrocene
Justin McBrien’s term for capitalism as a system that accumulates through extinction – the ‘new death’ of species, cultures and ecosystems on which its growth depends.
Negative Externalities
Costs of an activity borne by those outside it, rather than by the actors who choose to undertake it.
Nomadism
A way of life without a fixed home, moving with the seasons, food sources and herds – the norm for hunter-gatherer societies.
Novacene
James Lovelock’s name for a coming age in which hyper-intelligent machines, born of AI, succeed humanity as the planet’s dominant form of intelligence.
Original Affluent Society
Hunter-gatherer societies, understood as affluent in a minimalist sense: their needs were few and readily met by abundant natural resources.
Path Dependence
The way early choices constrain later ones, so that where a society can go depends on the route it has already taken.
Philosophy of Technology
The branch of philosophy that examines the nature of technology and its effects on human life and society – how tools shape the people who make and use them, and how they might be governed.
Political Economy
The social structures through which a society organises production, distribution and power.
Post-Industrial
Describing an economy in which services, information and digital technologies have overtaken manufacturing as the main sources of value.
Power-Elite
The small group of decision-makers whose local responses to competitive pressures enact – or restrict – the civilisation-wide diffusion of a new technology stack.
Private Property
Resources owned and controlled by individuals or groups to the exclusion of others – made possible by storable surplus.
Red Queen Race
An arms race in which each side must keep improving merely to hold its place against the others – after the Red Queen in Through the Looking-Glass, who must run as fast as she can to stay where she is.
Science and Technology Studies
The field that studies how science and technology shape society, and how society in turn shapes them.
Scientific Revolution
The replacement, in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, of inherited authority by observation, experiment and mathematics as the way to know nature – laying the groundwork for industry and the Enlightenment.
Security Dilemma
The predicament in which one state’s efforts to make itself secure – arming, allying, fortifying – make its rivals less secure, so that they respond in kind and all end up less safe.
Selection Pressure
A force in the environment that favours some traits or strategies over others, so that they spread while the rest decline.
Shared Myth
A story held in common – of gods, ancestors, nations or money – that lets people who have never met trust and cooperate with one another, and so act together in numbers far beyond the reach of kinship.
Social Construction of Technology
The view, set against technological determinism, that technologies are shaped by the social groups who design, contest and use them, rather than following an inevitable logic of their own.
Socio-Economic Class
A group defined by its position in the division of labour and its share of wealth and power – created, with inequality, by the agricultural surplus.
Socio-Technical System
A society understood as a single configuration of its technologies and the social structures built around them.
Spiral Model
An account of how conflict escalates through the security dilemma: each side reads the other’s defensive moves as a threat and answers in kind, so that mutual fear spirals towards arms races and war.
Status-Seeking
Behaviour aimed at raising one’s standing relative to others – with wealth-accumulating and power-gaming, part of the cultural psychology of competition.
Storable Surplus
Food and goods produced beyond immediate needs that can be kept over time – the basis of private property, trade and class.
Strategic Advantage
A lasting edge over rivals – military, economic or political – conferred by a new technology or form of organisation.
Super-Structural Change
A shift in a society’s culture, beliefs and psychology that follows from a change in its technology stack and social structures.
Superstructure
The culture, beliefs, religion and psychology of a society, which rest on and are reshaped by its technology stack and social structures.
Systemic Existential Risk
An existential risk that arises not from a single catastrophe or bad actor but from the structure of a system as a whole – the interaction of competitive pressures, institutions and technologies that moves it, step by step, towards catastrophe.
Technium
Kevin Kelly’s term for the whole of technology taken as a single, self-reinforcing system, which grows and evolves with tendencies of its own.
Techno-Capitalism
The stage of capitalism in which technological innovation itself becomes the main source of profit and power, concentrating both in the firms that own the leading technologies.
Technological Determinism
The view that technology develops by its own logic and drives social change on its own – the view this article argues against.
Technological Revolution
From the late nineteenth century, the age of electricity, steel, chemistry and the internal combustion engine, which brought mass production, continental networks and, within a generation, industrialised total war.
Technology Stack
The layered infrastructure of technologies through which a society produces, communicates and organises itself.
Urban Revolution
From around six thousand years ago, the growth of farming towns into the first cities, bringing writing, bronze and monumental building, and with them the state and the market in proto-form.
World-System
The global network of economic and political relations, between states and regions, within which societies compete.
