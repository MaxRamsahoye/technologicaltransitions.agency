  1. Allan Dafoe

    On Technological Determinism: A Typology, Scope Conditions, and a Mechanism

    Science, Technology, & Human Values, 2015

    Dafoe sorts the many versions of technological determinism into a typology and asks under what conditions technology really does drive social change. His answer is a mechanism he calls military-economic adaptationism: in a competitive international system, societies that adopt technologies conferring military and economic advantage prosper, and those that do not are outcompeted, conquered or forced to follow. The article supplies the structural half of this project’s model – the pressure that pushes each new technology stack across civilisation.

  2. Daniel Schmachtenberger

    The Emergence Model

    Schmachtenberger describes a society as three interacting layers: its infrastructure, the technologies and material systems through which it meets its needs; its social structure, the institutions of politics, law and economics; and its superstructure, its shared values, beliefs and culture. A change in any layer pressures the others, but the deepest and most durable shifts begin in the infrastructure. The model gives this project its way of tracing how a new technology stack reshapes political economy and cultural psychology.

    • Ken Wilber

      Integral Theory

      Wilber’s four-quadrant map holds that any whole has interior and exterior, individual and collective dimensions – experience, behaviour, culture and systems – and that none can be reduced to the others. It underlies the Emergence Model’s insistence that technological, institutional and cultural change be read together.

    • Marvin Harris

      Cultural Materialism: The Struggle for a Science of Culture

      1979

      Harris divides every society into infrastructure, structure and superstructure, and argues that the material conditions of production and reproduction are the primary drivers of cultural change. It is the source of the Emergence Model’s three layers, and of the priority it gives to infrastructure.

      Source ↗

    New Economics, Part IV

    Schmachtenberger traces how the economy came to be thought of, and managed, as something separate from the ecology that sustains it – an abstraction in which natural resources appear as inputs and their depletion as externalities. That separation, he argues, is part of what makes endless growth on a finite planet seem possible, and it is one of the roots of the present metacrisis.

  3. Peter Joseph

    The New Human Rights Movement: Reinventing the Economy to End Oppression

    2017

    Joseph argues that poverty, inequality and declining public health are not natural features of human life but products of an outdated socio-economic orientation built on scarcity, competition and endless growth. Structural oppression, on this account, follows from the economic system itself rather than from individual prejudice alone. The book informs this project’s view of the market as a historical construction, and of its effects as open to change.

    Source ↗

  4. Robert Sapolsky

    Behave: The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst

    2017

    Sapolsky explains human behaviour across every timescale, from the neurons firing a second before an act to the evolutionary pressures of millions of years. Among the long-run influences he traces is the invention of poverty: how agriculture, surplus and hierarchy introduced inequality on a scale unknown to hunter-gatherers, with lasting effects on stress, health and behaviour. It grounds this project’s account of cultural-psychological adaptation in biology.

  5. Jared Diamond

    The Worst Mistake in the History of the Human Race

    Discover, 1987

    Diamond argues that the adoption of agriculture, long celebrated as humanity’s decisive step forward, was in many ways a catastrophe: it brought class, inequality, malnutrition and disease, making elites better off and most people worse off. The essay frames this project’s account of the Agricultural Revolution.

    Source ↗

    Guns, Germs, and Steel: The Fates of Human Societies

    1997

    Diamond asks why some societies came to dominate others, and finds the answer in geography, domestication and the technologies and diseases they produced – an account of how technological advantage reshapes the world.

    Source ↗

  6. Marshall Sahlins

    The Original Affluent Society

    In Stone Age Economics, 1972

    Sahlins overturns the image of hunter-gatherers as living on the edge of starvation. Their wants were few and readily met, he argues, and they worked fewer hours than farmers or industrial workers: affluent not through abundance of goods but through the modesty of their needs. The essay underpins this project’s account of hunter-gatherer society as affluent in a minimalist sense, and of scarcity as something agriculture created.

    Source ↗

  7. Luke Kemp

    Goliath’s Curse: The History and Future of Societal Collapse

    2025

    Kemp studies the rise and collapse of more than four hundred societies across five thousand years, and finds a recurring pattern: dominance hierarchies – “Goliaths” – grow by extraction and status competition among elites until they become fragile and fall, while more equal and democratic societies prove more resilient. He then asks what that history means for a globally connected civilisation armed with technologies of mass destruction. It sharpens this project’s question of whether the power-elite will steer the Intelligence Revolution towards or away from catastrophe.
