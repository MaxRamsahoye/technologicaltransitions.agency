Technological Transitions

    Max Ramsahoye

    A Critical Evolutionary Game-Theory Model of Major Technological Transitions

    The civilisation-wide diffusion of a new tech stack is not predetermined by competitive pressures, but is enacted by the power-elite’s response to them

    Overview

    Our advanced, global civilisation is the result of a progressive series of technological revolutions – agricultural, industrial and digital – each fundamentally changing the political economy and cultural psychology of human populations, affording new strategic advantages and reshaping the fitness landscape of civilisation as a whole.

    So far civilisation has undergone three such major technological transitions. We are now in the midst of the fourth (and likely final): the Intelligence Revolution. The underlying structural dynamic driving it – military-economic adaptation, competitive pressures, multi-polar traps, arms races – is continuous with all preceding transitions. However, as an exponential technology, AI could amplify the effects of pre-existing incentives and generate negative externalities magnitudes more powerful and catastrophic, potentially even existential.

    This article revisits the foundational civilisational problem of inter-group competition and capability acceleration, drawing on the literature of science and technology studies, in concert with case studies of previous technological revolutions throughout history. Principally, the article proposes a critical evolutionary game-theory model of major technological transitions: as structurally driven by global competitive pressures and also agentially enacted by the local decision-making of the power elite, the ruling technocratic classes of each era, since complex, hierarchical civilisations began.

    Ultimately, it is an open question whether pro-human intentions can prevail over the dynamics and agents of the current world-system. As E.P Thompson insightfully put it: “If Man is ever obliterated from the earth by means of his own armaments, there will be no simple answer to the question: Did he fall, or was he pushed?”.

    Major Technological Transitions

    Whilst it is all we have ever known, the advanced, global, technological civilisation of today has only been in existence for a very brief period of human history. The society or socio-technical system, that we live within is a highly novel configuration, the large-scale culmination of a series of technological revolutions. So far, civilisation has undergone three such revolutions – Agricultural, Industrial and Digital – and we are now in the midst of a fourth (and likely final) one: the Intelligence Revolution.

    We call these major technological transitions: periods of time where the introduction of a new form of technology stack (infrastructure) fundamentally changes the wider political economy (social structures) and cultural psychology (superstructures) of the populations that deploy it, conferring competitive advantage and ultimately reshaping the fitness landscape of civilisation as a whole.

    The Agricultural Revolution

    To understand this we can turn back to history. The clearest case – and the most consequential (as well as the earliest chronologically) – was the first major technological transition from nomadic hunter-gatherer tribes to settled agrarian civilisations. In the next section, we set out the super-structural change that occurred and then the evolutionary game-theoretic dynamics that caused it.

    Structural & Super-Structural Change

    For 99% of human history, humans lived in small-scale groups (with social cognition capped below <150, now known as the ‘Dunbar number’) of nomadic hunter-gatherers: moving from place to place with seasonal cycles, the availability of foraging food sources and the migration patterns of wild game.

    With access to abundant natural food sources (material conditions) and to the tools, such as sacks and spears, to obtain it (means of production), tribes were able to subsist and provide for themselves.

    Due to the inherent perishability of gathered plants and hunted meats, resources were consumed in a short span of time after acquisition and shared where there was excess that would otherwise go to waste.

    Combined these factors meant that hunter-gatherer societies were affluent in a minimalist sense (‘the original affluent society’) and mostly egalitarian economically (and therefore politically and culturally too).

    Approximately between 12,000 and 4000 years ago (varying across geographical locations) civilisation(s) developed a set of innovations in its tech stack that led to a fundamental shift in its mode of economic production and political organisation and in turn cultural worldview (pre-rational egalitarian religions of animism/paganism to organised patriarchal religions of anthropocentric dominion).

    The domestication of plants and animals, along with irrigation, food storage, and later the plough, gave rise to geographically-bounded food production, storable surplus and in turn private property, its defence in the form of city-states, law, police and militaries, and its exchange in the form of trade, currency, merchants and markets. In other words, the co-evolutionary emergence of the state and the market in proto-form and their associated legal apparatus.

    This in turn resulted in the creation of socio-economic class, inequality, poverty and artificial scarcity. And also an internal cultural psychology of competitiveness, domination and fear manifest as wealth-accumulating (commonly moralistically labelled as ‘greed’), power-gaming (Machiavellianism) and status-seeking behaviour.

    Overall, the structural formation that anthropologists now define as civilisation: complex hierarchical societies with centralised decision-making (political power) and a division of labour (economic specialisation). The construction we still have today, albeit in a more elaborated, institutional form and with more advanced technologies operating it (a post-industrial tech stack).
